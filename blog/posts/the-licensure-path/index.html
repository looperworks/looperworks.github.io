The Licensure Path: ARE, AXP, and Whether It Actually Matters

Everyone tells you to get licensed. Not everyone tells you what that actually involves, how long it takes, or whether it's the right move for every career path.

You're sitting at a firm mixer, trying to figure out what to say about your career beyond "uh, I like buildings," and someone inevitably asks: "Are you going for your license?" The question lands the same way every time — with a slight assumption that of course you are. That getting licensed is just part of the checklist. Pass college, get a job, log your hours, pass your exams, become a "real" architect.

Except nobody actually explains what that means. Or how long it takes. Or, here's the kicker, whether it's actually necessary for the thing you want to do with your career.

Let's fix that.

What Licensure Actually Means

First, let's demystify the jargon, because architects are very good at making simple things sound complicated.

Licensure isn't one test. It's a multi-year process with three main components: AXP (the experience requirement), ARE (the exams), and state registration. You can't skip any of them. You also can't do them in reverse order, or all at once, or while holding down a normal job and maintaining a social life and still sleeping sometimes. (That last one is technically possible but not recommended.)

Here's the thing: licensure is a years-long process that runs parallel to your early career. It's not something you do after you've "made it." You start it the day you graduate. Or really, the day you get your first job. And depending on where you live and how efficiently you move through the system, you won't hold the letters after your name until you're somewhere between 30 and 35.

Let's walk through what that actually looks like.

AXP: The Experience You're Already Getting (You Just Need to Log It)

AXP stands for "Architectural Experience Program." It used to be called IDP (Intern Development Program), and if you're talking to someone licensed before 2023, they'll probably still call it that. Language in architecture evolves slowly.

Here's what AXP is: a requirement to log somewhere between 3,000 and 5,600 hours of work experience in defined categories before you can sit for the ARE. The variation depends on your state and your educational background. If you have a professional degree (5-year B.Arch), you need fewer hours. If you have a non-professional degree, you need more.

The hours are organized into six areas of practice: Programming and Analysis, Project Planning and Design, Project Management and Coordination, Contracts and Documentation, Project Delivery and Administration, and Practice Management. You need a certain minimum in each category, and you need to document everything meticulously because the NCARB (National Council of Architectural Registration Boards — yes, there's a board for this) is going to review your submissions and they do not accept "I think I did this stuff."

But here's the good news: most of what you do in an architecture office counts toward AXP. You don't need special projects. You don't need to work on a certain type of building. The kid fresh out of school doing drawing sets for residential additions is logging hours just as legitimately as someone working on a 500-person office tower.

The biggest mistake people make is not logging hours from day one. You think you'll get to it later. You think you'll remember what you did and how many hours you spent. Spoiler alert: you won't. The people who sail through AXP are the ones who track their hours consistently, year after year, in real time. It takes about five minutes a week to stay current.

If you slack off and only start tracking in year three? You'll spend months reconstructing your work history and hoping you can justify what you've done.

Start logging your AXP hours on day one of your first job, even if licensure feels five years away. Future you will send present you a thank-you card.

ARE: Six Exams That Test Things You Forgot You Learned

The ARE (Architect Registration Examination) is a six-part exam administered by NCARB. There's PlanPrep, Programming and Analysis, Project Planning, Project Management, Project Delivery, and Practice Management. Yes, the names are repetitive. No, the tests don't get easier with the repetition.

Here's the real talk: the ARE is hard. Not impossible — plenty of people pass — but hard. Each division covers an enormous breadth of material, and the questions often aren't testing whether you're an expert in something. They're testing whether you know enough to not make a dangerous mistake. You don't need to memorize building code. You need to know that building codes exist and why they matter. You don't need to understand every structural system. You need to know enough about structures to coordinate with an engineer and know when something doesn't look right.

Most people take the exams over an 18-month to two-year period. If you try to take all six at once (and some optimists do), you'll probably fail at least one. If you try to cram for all of them simultaneously, you'll definitely fail at least one. The pass rate hovers around 60-70% depending on the division, which means a lot of smart, capable architects don't pass on their first try.

Here's what actually works: Take them in a reasonable sequence. Study groups are your friend — find three or four people at your office going through the same exams and meet weekly. Use practice exams obsessively. There are prep courses that are worth the money if you're the kind of person who actually goes to them. If you're just going to buy it and never open it, save the money.

You're allowed to retake any exam you fail, but each attempt costs money and time, so it's worth taking this seriously. The people who breeze through are usually the ones who studied for 8-10 weeks per exam and actually understood the material instead of just memorizing facts they'd forget immediately.

Does Getting Licensed Actually Matter?

Now we get to the honest question. And the honest answer is: it depends on what you want to do.

YES, you should get licensed if:

  • You want to put your stamp on drawings (literal stamp — there's a physical stamp involved, it's very official)
  • You're thinking about running your own firm someday
  • You want to work as a consultant or in a specialized role that requires you to be the person responsible for a project
  • You're planning to work internationally — many countries recognize the AIA and make reciprocity easier if you're already licensed

MAYBE YOU DON'T NEED IT if:

  • You're in computational design or digital architecture and have zero interest in stamping drawings
  • You want to specialize in visualization and you're happy being amazing at rendering
  • You're moving toward academia or research
  • You're in urban design, UX design, or strategy — areas where the architecture license matters way less than the quality of your thinking
  • You're planning to work in-house at a tech company or a non-traditional firm where no one's ever going to need an architect's stamp
  • You want to go into landscape architecture instead (different license, whole different path)

Here's the thing nobody wants to hear: getting your license doesn't make you a better designer. It makes you legally authorized to practice architecture independently in one specific jurisdiction. It's bureaucratic legitimacy, not creative credibility. You can be an incredible architect without the letters after your name. (You'll just have to work for someone who has the letters, or find a career path where the letters don't matter.)

The title "Architect" is legally protected. You can't call yourself an architect in most states unless you're licensed. But "architect" in the job title is different from "architect" in what you actually do. The person designing innovative climate solutions in the computational design department at a forward-thinking firm is doing architecture. They might not have a license. They might never get one. And they might be doing more interesting work than someone with a stamp is.

A Practical Timeline

Let's talk numbers. Here's what a realistic path looks like:

  • Year 1-3 of work: Logging AXP hours while working a regular job. You're not trying to do anything special. You're just documenting what you're already doing.
  • Year 3-4: You've logged enough hours to sit for the ARE. You start taking exams. Most people spread them out — two per year, or sometimes three in one year with one the next year.
  • Year 4-6: Still taking exams. Some people move faster, some slower. You're juggling work, maybe other certifications, maybe a life.
  • Year 6-7 (age 30-33 area): You've passed all six divisions. You're now eligible to apply for licensure in your state, which involves submitting all your AXP documentation and paying fees. A few months later, you're officially a licensed architect.

It's a marathon. Literally. Seven years from graduation to licensure is normal. If you do it in five, you're ahead of the curve. If it takes eight or nine, you're not unusual — you're just human.

The reality is this: licensure is a long-term commitment on top of an already demanding career in a profession that doesn't exactly treat junior staff gently. You'll be working long hours on projects, staying late to finish drawings, and also somehow finding time to study for exams. Meanwhile, people outside of architecture are watching TV and sleeping eight hours a night.

So before you commit to this path, ask yourself: Do I actually need this? Or am I doing it because it's what you're supposed to do?

If the answer is "I need this because I want to stamp drawings and run my own firm," then go all in. Track your hours obsessively, schedule your exams, join a study group, and treat it like a marathon. You'll get there.

If the answer is "I don't know, everyone just says I should," then maybe spend some time exploring whether your actual career goals require a stamp. Because the next seven years of your life should be in service of something you actually want, not something that sounded important at graduation.

Either way, you're not broken if you don't have a license. And you're not ahead just because you do. You're just taking a different path through an already complicated profession.

Choose your path intentionally.
