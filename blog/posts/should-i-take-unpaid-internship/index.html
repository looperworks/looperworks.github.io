Should I Take an Unpaid Internship?

In almost every case, no. And there are legal reasons why.

No. In almost every case, you should not take an unpaid internship. Unpaid internships in architecture are ethically questionable and often illegal under the Fair Labor Standards Act. If the firm benefits from your work, they should pay you.

It's a legal issue, not just an ethical one

The Fair Labor Standards Act requires payment when an internship displaces paid employees, generates immediate commercial advantage for the firm, or involves performing productive work. Most architecture internships meet these criteria. The firm is not paying you to learn. They are paying you to work and happen to be teaching you while you work.

When a firm offers an unpaid internship, they are asking you to work for free. That is not legal employment practice. It is not industry norm. It is a firm trying to save money.

The profession already underpays

Architecture graduates earn less than their education costs. The average architecture degree takes five or six years and costs significantly more than many other professions. Taking unpaid work reinforces this problem. It signals to the profession that we will work for free. That keeps wages low.

When you work unpaid, you also take an opportunity away from someone who needs to earn money. You make it easier for firms to not pay interns. You make it harder for other students to negotiate pay.

What counts as a legitimate exception

Very short academic externships tied to school credit are sometimes acceptable. One or two weeks where your school gives you credit and the firm is not relying on you for actual project work. That is different from a three-month unpaid internship doing real design work.

If the program is tied to academic credit, is very short, and you are explicitly there to learn rather than contribute, there is an argument for it. Everything else should be paid.

What to do instead

Apply broadly. Use the Threshold firm search to find firms with actual internship programs. Many firms offer paid internships. Some of the best firms in the country pay interns because they understand that talent costs money.

Be willing to relocate. Paid internships might require moving to a different city for the summer. That is worth it. It is better than staying in your hometown doing unpaid work.

If a firm offers unpaid work, decline. Tell them you are looking for a paid opportunity. Many firms will say yes if you push back. Some will not, and those are not firms you want to work for anyway.

The ripple effect

Every student who refuses unpaid work makes it harder for the next firm to offer it. Every firm that pays interns sets the standard higher. You have power here. Use it.
