Side Projects and Building Your Professional Identity

Your day job builds your skills. Your side projects build your reputation. Here's how to use competitions, writing, and social media to become visible in the profession.

Why Your 9-to-5 Isn't Enough

Let's get something straight: this isn't about hustle culture. This isn't about staying up until 2 AM because you haven't "earned" leisure time. This is about agency.

Your day job builds your skills. It teaches you how to work in production, how to navigate office politics, how to get a building built. Those things matter. But your firm's work is their portfolio, not yours. When you leave, the building stays on their website. You leave with experience and a check, which is the deal, but it's not a platform.

Side projects are different. They're the work that's unmistakably yours. They show what you care about when nobody's paying you to care. They're proof that you can initiate, that you can see something through, that you have taste and point of view. And in a profession where reputation compounds over time, that matters.

Competitions: Low Risk, High Signal

You probably won't win. That's the good news. It means you can stop thinking of competitions as lotteries and start thinking of them as structured projects.

Enter a competition and you get something your day job rarely gives you: a clear brief, a timeline, and the freedom to push in directions your firm would never consider. You explore a new typology. You collaborate with people you've never worked with. You solve problems that actually interest you, not the ones that happened to come through the studio door.

The winning scheme gets hung in a gallery. That's nice. But the real value is the work itself. Proof that you can conceive and execute an idea from zero. That's a portfolio piece. That's a story you can tell in interviews. That's a connection you made with your competition team that might become your next job.

Don't enter every open call. That's not a strategy, that's noise. Be selective. Pick competitions that genuinely appeal to you — the brief aligns with your interests, the program interests you, the location or cultural context resonates. Three thoughtful competition entries beat fifteen scattered applications to whatever's open.

Writing About Architecture

You don't need to be a critic. You don't need to have all the answers. You just need to write about what you're noticing and why it matters to you.

Blog posts. LinkedIn articles. Even thoughtful social media captions that go deeper than "love this detail." Writing clarifies thinking. It forces you to articulate why something works, why something failed, what's missing from the current discourse. And in a profession where most architects don't write, you immediately stand out.

Most architects don't write. That's your opportunity.

You've spent years learning to see — to notice proportions, materials, details. Writing is how you share that vision. And sharing it consistently, over months and years, positions you as someone who cares about the discourse. Not just the buildings. The thinking behind them.

Start small. A thoughtful reflection on a building you visited. A breakdown of a detail you loved. A question about why we design spaces the way we do. Make it conversational. Make it honest. The architects who build real platforms are the ones writing like they're talking to a friend at a desk crit, not like they're trying to impress someone in the room.

Social Media (Without Being Cringe)

Instagram, LinkedIn, maybe TikTok if you're genuinely interested. But please, whatever you do, don't treat it like a portfolio export.

Share work-in-progress. Sketch details that confused you until you figured it out. Site visits. Book recommendations. The small observations that show how you think about space. Most architects on social media are performing — filtered renders, inspirational quotes, the highlight reel. The ones who build genuine followings are doing something different. They're contributing. They're real.

Consistency matters more than frequency. One thoughtful post a week beats fifteen random ones. Interact with other architects' work. Ask questions in comments. Start conversations. Social media is infrastructure for your network, and networks grow through genuine engagement, not broadcast.

Freelance and Visualization Work

Money's nice. New skills are nicer. If you can pick up freelance work — renderings, visualization, architecture tech tools — that's income and portfolio expansion simultaneously.

Start small. A friend's renovation. Renderings for a competition team. Nothing that conflicts with your day job (check your employment agreement first). Build a side practice thoughtfully, not frantically. The goal isn't to replace your day job; it's to have another way to develop skills and make work that's uniquely yours.

The pros: you build relationships, you learn tools faster than any workshop could teach, you have leverage in your day job conversations. "I have options" changes how you negotiate everything.

The cons: time management is brutal, and if your employment agreement says "everything you design belongs to us," you need to understand what that actually means. Read it. Ask a lawyer if it's unclear. Respect boundaries. Your side work shouldn't become a resentment machine.

The Compound Effect

None of this is revolutionary. This is slow, consistent work. Three years from now, you'll have competition entries, a few published pieces, maybe 500 genuine followers who actually engage with your work, and a body of side projects that show depth and range. That architect — the one with accumulated proof of serious thinking — has a completely different trajectory than the one with only their day job to show.

Opportunities compound on visibility. A gallery sees your competition work and invites you to exhibit. A magazine finds your writing and asks you to contribute. A follower becomes a client. A collaborator becomes a partner. These connections don't happen to architects who only show up in their firms' projects.

This is how you build a reputation. Not overnight. In five-year chunks. Not through hustle. Through accumulated, intentional work that's genuinely yours.
